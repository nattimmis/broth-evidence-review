# 01 — Hepatotoxicity of the proposed ingredients

Both headline "medicinal" ingredients in the original brief have documented
liver-injury signals. Neither is dangerous as a *spice*. Both become a real problem
at the doses and potentiated forms that wellness protocols recommend — which is
exactly what was being proposed.

## Turmeric / curcumin

Turmeric-associated drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is documented and increasing.
The Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network published a ten-case series under the title
*"Liver Injury Associated with Turmeric — A Growing Problem"*. Injury is
predominantly **hepatocellular**.

### The piperine problem

This is the part that matters, and it inverts the usual folk advice.

The standard wellness instruction is "take turmeric with black pepper, because
piperine boosts absorption." That is pharmacologically true — roughly **20 mg of
piperine can increase curcumin bioavailability ~20-fold** — and it is precisely the
mechanism implicated in the injury cases. Recent reports are dominated by
supplements *formulated with added piperine*.

Proposed mechanism is multifactorial:

- **Immune-mediated component** — piperine exposure plus the **HLA-B\*35:01** allele
  is noted in affected patients, suggesting a genetic susceptibility that cannot be
  predicted in advance for any given person.
- **Dose-dependent pro-oxidant flip** — piperine inhibits cytochrome enzymes and
  raises systemic curcumin; at high concentrations curcumin shifts from antioxidant
  to **pro-oxidant**, generating reactive oxygen species and inducing hepatocyte
  apoptosis.

### Prognosis

Reassuring, and worth stating plainly: in reported cases liver tests normalised
after **discontinuing the supplement**. The drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis
phenotype usually resolves within ~6 months without long-term immunosuppression and
rarely progresses to cirrhosis.

### Practical position

| Form | Verdict |
|------|---------|
| Turmeric as a culinary spice in food | Reasonable |
| Normal black pepper in cooking | Reasonable — different order of magnitude from a supplement |
| Turmeric extract / curcumin supplement | Avoid in liver disease |
| Anything with **added piperine** or "enhanced bioavailability" | Avoid — this is the implicated pattern |
| "Golden paste" megadose protocols | Avoid |

## Cinnamon — the species is the whole story

"Cinnamon" is two different things sold under one word, and the difference is
toxicologically enormous.

| | Coumarin content | Relative |
|---|---|---|
| **Cassia** (*Cinnamomum cassia*) — the cheap default | ~1% by mass | baseline |
| **Ceylon / "true"** (*C. verum*) | ~0.004% by mass | **~250x lower** |

Coumarin hepatotoxicity is dose-dependent and idiosyncratic. That combination is the
basis for the **EFSA tolerable daily intake of 0.1 mg/kg body weight** — about
**7 mg/day for a 70 kg adult**.

### The numbers are not comfortable

- **One teaspoon** of ground cassia at average coumarin concentration contains
  **7–20 mg coumarin** — already at or above the daily limit for most adults, from a
  single teaspoon.
- At **6 g/day of cassia for four weeks**, coumarin intake exceeds the EFSA TDI. At
  the same dose of **Ceylon, it does not**.
- Case report: a **23-year-old hospitalised with hepatitis** after 1–2 g cinnamon
  daily for two months — roughly 3.3–6.6 mg coumarin/day at cassia concentrations.
  That is a *culinary-scale* dose causing clinical injury.

### Practical position

Use **Ceylon** and verify the species. Supermarket "cinnamon" with no species named
is cassia by default. Ceylon sticks are soft, layered and crumbly like a cigar;
cassia is a single hard, thick curl. A single Ceylon stick in a 4 L pot, added for
the last 90 minutes, is a non-issue.
