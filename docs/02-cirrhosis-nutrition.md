# 02 — What the nutrition guidelines actually say

This is where the real leverage is. Three of these findings are counterintuitive and
one of them is routinely missed by patients and families.

## 1. Protein goes UP, not down

The single most important correction. **The old dogma of restricting protein in
liver disease has been overturned.** It persists in public understanding — and
occasionally in well-meaning advice from relatives — long after the guidelines moved.

EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines on nutrition in chronic liver disease:

| Population | Protein target |
|---|---|
| Stable cirrhosis | **1.2–1.5 g/kg IBW/day** |
| Cirrhosis with sarcopenia | **1.5 g/kg IBW/day** |
| Critically ill | 1.2–2.0 g/kg IBW/day |

Why it matters: **sarcopenia is a hard prognostic factor** in cirrhosis, not a
cosmetic concern. Contributors include increased skeletal muscle ammonia, reduced
testosterone and growth hormone, endotoxaemia, and simple inadequate intake. Muscle
is also where ammonia gets handled when the liver can't — so losing muscle worsens
the encephalopathy that protein restriction was historically meant to prevent.

The same target range applies to cancer cachexia, where muscle wasting is likewise
prognostic. Both conditions point the same direction: **more protein, not less.**

## 2. The late-evening snack

Specifically recommended, widely skipped, and probably the highest-value item in
this entire repo.

A cirrhotic liver holds little glycogen. An overnight fast therefore flips the body
into a catabolic state relatively quickly, burning muscle for fuel — the effect of
an ordinary night's sleep on a cirrhotic patient has been compared to a much longer
fast in a healthy person. A carbohydrate-and-protein snack before bed shortens that
window.

EASL recommends it. Research notes patients **often miss it despite its importance**.
It costs nothing and requires no prescription.

## 3. Broth is not a protein source

The uncomfortable finding for the whole premise.

Bone broth protein is **collagen**. Under PDCAAS — the standard protein quality
method — collagen **lacks tryptophan entirely** and is therefore classed as an
**incomplete protein**. It is also low in methionine, and low in the
**branched-chain amino acids** that carry specific evidence in cirrhosis.

What broth *is* rich in: glycine, proline, hydroxyproline. Pleasant, useful for gut
comfort and hydration, and responsible for the gelatin set — but not the amino acids
that build muscle.

**Conclusion: use broth as a vehicle.** Its real value is that a person with no
appetite will drink a warm mug when they will not face a plate. Load it with
complete protein and calories and it becomes genuinely valuable. Serve it alone and
it is, nutritionally, close to hot water with personality.

## 4. Sodium — and the trap inside the sodium advice

With ascites, the target is **<2 g sodium/day** (≈90 mEq/day, ≈5.2 g of salt).
Even modest sodium intake can drive a positive sodium balance and ascites formation,
because the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, sympathetic nervous system and
ADH are all pushing sodium and water retention.

A 13-hour broth, reduced and salted in the pot, is exactly the wrong vehicle —
canned and prepared broths are called out by name as high-sodium items to avoid.
**Hence: no salt in the pot, salt at the bowl.** The cook controls the ceiling; the
person controls the taste.

### The trap

Guidelines flag it explicitly and it deserves emphasis: **sodium restriction reduces
palatability, which can reduce intake, which worsens malnutrition.** Clinicians are
advised to be cautious about nutritional status in sodium-restricted patients, and
**liberalisation should be considered in patients who are malnourished** on a
restricted diet.

So the two goals are in genuine tension. The resolution is culinary rather than
clinical: build flavour from **acid, aromatics, herbs, alliums, fat and browning** —
lemon, garlic, parsley, good olive oil, deeply roasted bones — so the food stays
worth eating at low sodium. That is the entire reason the recipe roasts the bones
hard and finishes with lemon and herbs. Where the tension is unresolvable, **the
dietitian decides, not the cook.**
