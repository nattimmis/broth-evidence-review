# 05 — Antioxidant foods, audited

The premise under test: that greens, blueberries and other high-antioxidant foods
help clear something out of a failing liver. They do not. But one ordinary drink in
this category has better evidence than anything else people eat, and it is worth
separating the two.

## Coffee — the one that actually holds up

The strongest food-level signal in hepatology, and it survives in decaffeinated,
instant and ground coffee alike, which means the effect is not simply caffeine.

| Outcome | Effect | Cohort |
|---|---|---|
| Death from chronic liver disease | **HR 0.51** | UK Biobank, n=494,585 |
| Death from cirrhosis | **RR 0.55** per extra 2 cups/day | meta-analysis |
| Developing cirrhosis | **RR 0.56** per extra 2 cups/day | 9 studies, n=432,133 |
| Developing **alcohol-related** cirrhosis | **RR 0.62** per extra 2 cups/day | the relevant subgroup |
| Hepatocellular carcinoma | **RR 0.60** | 16 studies, 3,153 cases |
| Having significant fibrosis | **RR 0.65** | 5 studies, n=4,303 |
| HCC, prospective cohort | HR 0.80 — **not significant** | UK Biobank |

**The honest caveat:** every one of these is observational. No randomised trial of
coffee has ever used a hard liver endpoint, so reverse causation — people who feel
ill stop drinking coffee — is not excluded. This is a strong reason to keep drinking
coffee. It is not proof that starting will save a liver.

## Berries and anthocyanins — null

Anthocyanins are the pigment in blueberries and dark berries, and they have been
properly tested.

- Meta-analysis of **15 RCTs, 1,028 participants**, doses 0.77–640 mg/day over
  28–90 days: **no significant effect on ALT, AST or GGT.** Every liver enzyme
  endpoint was null.
- Largest single fatty-liver trial (n=74, 320 mg/day, 12 weeks): no significant
  difference in anthropometrics or plasma lipids.
- **No human berry trial has ever measured fibrosis or survival.**

Berries remain excellent food — fibre, potassium, vitamin C, and they make a bowl of
porridge worth finishing. They are not a liver intervention.

## Vegetables and tea — real but modest

| Exposure | Effect | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetables, per 100 g/day → liver cancer | HR 0.83 | EPIC European cohort |
| Brewed green tea → liver cancer | RR 0.88 | n=465,274, Asian cohorts only |
| Green leafy vegetables → HCC | HR 0.59 (0.35–1.01) | **crosses 1.0**, only 101 cases |

## Where the antioxidant story runs out

- **Cochrane review, 20 RCTs, 1,225 participants** (beta-carotene, vitamins A/C/E,
  selenium): no effect on all-cause or liver-related mortality, and antioxidants
  significantly *raised* GGT.
- **PIVENS** (vitamin E 800 IU/day, 96 weeks, n=247, biopsy-proven): inflammation
  improved, **fibrosis did not**. This is the largest antioxidant trial in liver
  disease with actual biopsies and it was negative for the endpoint that matters.

## Antioxidant supplements during chemotherapy

Distinct from food, and worth stating separately. In **1,134 breast cancer patients**,
antioxidant supplement use both before *and* during chemotherapy carried a recurrence
hazard ratio of **1.41** (95% CI 0.98–2.04, p=0.06) — not statistically significant,
but pointing the wrong way, with no demonstrated benefit on the other side of the
scale. American Cancer Society position: obtain antioxidants **from food, not
supplements**.

**Food-level intake carries none of this concern.** No guideline restricts fruit,
vegetables, coffee or tea during chemotherapy.

## Green tea extract — a genuine hazard

Brewed green tea is fine. Concentrated **extract** is rated category A by the NIH
liver-injury database — a well-established cause of clinically apparent liver injury,
with over 100 published cases including acute liver failure, transplant and death.
HLA-B\*35:01 appears in 72% of cases versus 11–15% of controls, so susceptibility
cannot be predicted in advance.
