# 04 — The cancer and cirrhosis claims, audited

## Curcumin and cancer

Curcumin is one of the most-studied natural compounds in oncology. After all of it,
the critical analysis of clinical trials in relation to bioavailability and malignant
disease concludes that studies **"failed to convincingly demonstrate that curcumin
has a significant, positive effect on the therapy of malignant diseases"**, and that
**"at the time being, the use of curcumin in malignant diseases is not justified
scientifically."**

The literature is not uniformly negative — some reviews describe curcumin as holding
promise — but the methodological picture explains the gap. Published trials
frequently have **small sample sizes, no randomisation, and non-standardised
curcumin formulations**, which destroys comparability and reproducibility.

### The bioavailability wall

The mechanistic reason the lab results don't translate. Curcumin has **poor oral
bioavailability, rapid metabolism and limited stability**. Phase I work established
it is safe in humans even at **12 g/day** — an enormous dose — while *still*
achieving low bioavailability. Something that cannot reach the tumour in meaningful
concentration cannot act on it, however impressive its behaviour in a dish.

Note the trap in the fix: formulations claiming enhanced bioavailability show
positive results in work close to the supplement industry, but **independent studies
could not reproduce the increase**. And the bioavailability enhancer of choice —
piperine — is the compound implicated in liver injury (see
[`01-hepatotoxicity.md`](01-hepatotoxicity.md)). The two problems are the same problem.

### Interaction risk

If the person is on chemotherapy, supplements must be disclosed to the oncologist.
High-dose antioxidants and CYP-inhibiting compounds can plausibly interfere with the
drugs doing the actual work. A spice in food is not the concern; a concentrated
extract taken alongside treatment is.

**Verdict: safe as a spice, not a treatment.** Nothing in the recipe depends on it
working — the turmeric is there for colour, warmth and flavour, which it genuinely
delivers.

## "Reversing" cirrhosis — the honest version

This one is more hopeful than a flat refusal suggests, so it is worth stating
carefully.

**Liver fibrosis can genuinely regress.** This is well documented and is not fringe.
What drives it is **removing the cause**:

| Aetiology | The lever that actually works |
|---|---|
| Alcohol-related | Cessation |
| Hepatitis B / C | Antiviral therapy |
| MASLD / MASH (fatty liver) | Weight loss, metabolic control |

Nutrition **supports the body while those work**. It is not itself the lever. No
food, spice, broth or cooking duration has been shown to reverse established
fibrosis.

**So the useful question is not "what should they eat to reverse it" but "is the
underlying cause identified and being treated."** If there is a treatable aetiology
here, that conversation with the hepatologist is worth more than everything else in
this repository combined.

## Bone marrow and "liver cleansing"

Marrow is **mostly fat**. That has real value for someone malnourished: dense
calories in a small, palatable volume, which is exactly the constraint when appetite
has collapsed.

There is **no cleansing mechanism**. The liver is not a filter that accumulates
residue to be flushed; "detox" and "cleansing" have no physiological referent here.
Marrow does nothing to fibrosis.

Two caveats: marrow is very rich, and may be poorly tolerated where there is fat
malabsorption or cholestasis. And the lead consideration in
[`03-food-safety.md`](03-food-safety.md) applies most directly to bone-derived foods.

**Verdict: a good calorie source for someone who needs calories. Nothing more, and
nothing more is needed for it to be worth serving.**
